Situation

Five tools, none of them agreeing

"What is this order really making us?" Nobody could answer without a day of reconciliation.

The Pattern House website was bringing in good inquiries. Then each one disappeared into WhatsApp threads. Quotes were worked out in a separate calculator, deals tracked in a CRM, orders in a spreadsheet and receipts on paper.

Follow-ups depended on someone remembering. When we traced the numbers by hand, quotes were underpriced and a running total typed into a cell had already caused an over-purchase of stock.

Task

One system from first inquiry to cash

I owned this end to end: the problem, the product decisions, the design, the build and the release. My brief to myself was simple. Make winning a client faster, and make every number trustworthy.

That meant replacing all five tools at once, for a team of sales, production and floor staff who would use it every day, without stopping the factory.

The mission
  • Win clients faster with one pipeline, named owners and follow-up that runs itself
  • Price every quote from real cost rates, so each order shows what it makes
  • Run sales, production, stock and money in one place
  • Switch over from the spreadsheet without losing a single kobo

What I did, step by step

1

Redesigned the path from lead to client

I rebuilt the sales workflow around clear stages and a named owner at every stage. Moving a deal forward sends the right email, delivers the SOP and SLA, and sets the next follow-up automatically.

A lead becomes a client the moment the first payment lands, in the same step that opens the order and records the receipt.

Why it mattered: nothing waits on someone's memory, so deals stop stalling between stages.

2

Wrote the money and stock rules before any screen

I treated the arithmetic as the product. Before design started, I wrote one binding decision record for how stock and money behave, so every engineer built against the same rules.

Prices are derived from cost-rate settings, never typed. Stock levels are calculated from an immutable ledger, never stored as a running total, because a stored total is exactly what caused the over-purchase.

Why it mattered: the studio finally knows what each order makes before saying yes to it.
3

Put the whole factory in one platform

Sales, quotes and invoices, orders, production planning, quality control, inventory and purchasing, finance and marketing now share one database and one source of truth.

Buying works the way the studio actually buys: production raises a request, an admin approves and pays, someone buys at the market with a receipt photo, and stock is received against the order.

Why it mattered: one place to quote, invoice, plan, inspect, buy and close the month.

4

Protected the money by role

Three roles, Admin, Production and Floor, plus a separate finance-access switch. Order value, payments and margin are removed on the server for anyone without it, not just hidden on screen.

Every change is written to an audit log in the same transaction, and the build fails if any write skips it.

Why it mattered: floor staff get the tool they need without seeing what they shouldn't.
5

Switched over without losing a kobo

The old spreadsheet held every lead, order and receipt since the studio opened, with duplicates and totals that had drifted. I would not move the business on an import that was "close".

The import had to reconcile every receipt to the kobo before it could touch production, and it was rehearsed until it passed with zero stops. Every record then came across in a single release window and verified one to one.

Why it mattered: the team trusted the new system from day one.
The release window
  • Rehearse the import on copies until zero stops
  • Freeze the spreadsheet
  • Back up the database
  • Migrate, import, verify one to one
  • Release. The sheet becomes read-only history

Behind the scenesMore product decisions and the lesson I took away
Statuses

Behaviour lives in flags, not names

Whether a status reserves stock or counts toward money is a flag, so renaming a status cannot silently break the maths.

Dates

Derive dates the client never sees

The production deadline is five working days before the client's due date, calculated on the server from one stored date.

Design

Copy proven layouts, not code

When the first leads screen fell flat, I had the team map each screen to established CRM patterns and rebuild in the studio's own look.

Cost

Spend nothing new on infrastructure

It runs as a second project in the existing hosting team with its own database. Incremental cost: zero per month.

Lesson: green ticks are not evidence.

At the first user acceptance round, more than three hundred unit tests were passing and the floor still found 33 problems, because nothing clicked a button and CI was not running the tests. A quarter of the test files checked that code looked right, not that it worked. Now I ask what a test actually exercises before I count it.
